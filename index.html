About

Public finance researcher working on the fiscal architecture of intergovernmental programs.

Biography

I completed my PhD in Public Administration and Policy in 2025, following an M.P.P. and a B.A. in economics. I work as an independent scholar and am not affiliated with any university or agency.

My research asks why programs financed jointly by federal and state governments so often drift toward insolvency. Unemployment insurance is the central case: states set their own tax and benefit parameters inside a federal framework, and the resulting incentives determine whether trust funds are ready for the next recession. My dissertation traced how states diverged in rebuilding those funds after the Great Recession, and whether the 2010 federal reform actually improved solvency.

Related strands of my work examine how fiscal and geographic factors drive policy diffusion across local governments, and how institutions use rhetoric to preserve costly commitments over long periods. Alongside the academic work, I build interactive tools that make public policy data tractable for people who are not statisticians, and write PoliMetrics, a newsletter of original data analysis.

Forthcoming Article

When the Market for Religion Fails: Tiebout Sorting, Mobility Assumptions, and the Limits of Religious Economy Theory

Journal of Church and State, vol. 70, no. 2 (Spring 2028)

The religious economy framework holds that competition among religious groups serves adherents better than state regulation would, and its structure follows closely that of Charles Tiebout's model of household sorting across competing local jurisdictions. This article makes the parallel explicit and applies to religious markets the four conditions under which Tiebout equilibria are held to fail — mobility costs, endogenous preferences, thin markets, and information asymmetries — testing each against sociological, ethnographic, and disaffiliation research.

Findings. Because exit costs are modest and switching routine for most adherents of most traditions, the failure conditions bind in proportion to a group's position on the church-sect continuum rather than as general properties of religious markets. Five propositions specify where competitive sorting should and should not yield the outcomes the framework predicts, with implications for religious exemptions from generally applicable law, children's rights within religious communities, financial disclosure by tax-exempt religious organizations, and protections for members who disaffiliate.

Forthcoming Article

Preserving Policy Through Narrative Transformation: A Computational Analysis of Mormon Tithing Discourse, 1850–2024

Journal of the Mormon Social Science Association

How do institutions keep a costly requirement in place for 175 years without changing it? Using large language models and fine-tuned DistilBERT classifiers on 4,002 general conference passages, I measure three rhetorical dimensions of Mormon tithing discourse — commandment versus opportunity, fear versus love, and material versus spiritual emphasis — to test Armand Mauss's optimum tension theory.

Findings. Rhetoric cycles between authoritarian retrenchment and assimilationist appeal, tracking financial crises, federal persecution, and economic downturns. Institutions can preserve demanding practices through coordinated narrative evolution rather than policy change.
