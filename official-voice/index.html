U.S. government messaging on Twitter, X and Truth Social · 2009–2026

Official Voice Tracker

How the President and the federal government speak to the public: 254,082 posts from Trump's own accounts, the White House, the State Department and 70 agency and official accounts, each scored on six rhetorical dimensions and a Fascist Rhetoric Index.

The President's own voice broke institutional norms first. In his second term, the institutions followed.

Trump's own posts have sat well below every government account on the norms index since 2009, and holding office did not change that. Government accounts held their register through his first term. In the second, the same accounts became less professional, more partisan and more leader-centered, and began to use rhetoric with fascist markers that had been almost entirely his.

The Fascist Rhetoric Index (FRI) scores each post on seven rhetorical markers drawn from scholarly definitions of fascism (Paxton, Griffin, Eco, Stanley, and Levitsky and Ziblatt). It measures rhetoric. It does not say whether any person, party or government is fascist, and every marker also appears in ordinary politics. Scholars stress that fascism is a syndrome, so the headline measure is the share of syndrome posts: posts with three or more markers, at least one of them a threat-to-democracy marker (enemy or plot, dehumanization, anti-democratic delegitimization or violence). The model is never fully certain a marker is absent, so posts with none still score about 0.1–0.5; read differences against that floor.

The seven markers

Each marker is scored 0 (absent), 1 (present) or 2 (strong). The index is 100 × the sum of the seven scores ÷ 14. The full codebook, with coding rules and examples, is govcomm/CODEBOOK_FASCISM.md on the data branch.

Decline and rebirthThe nation has decayed or been betrayed and must be restored; "make … great again"; a golden age reborn. Griffin's palingenetic myth; Stanley's mythic past.
Existential enemy or plotDomestic opponents or institutions cast as a threat to the nation, as traitors or "enemy of the people", or as a rigged, deep-state conspiracy. Paxton; Eco's obsession with a plot.
Dehumanization or purityAn outgroup cast as collectively criminal, an "invasion", or in terms of vermin, animals, poison or contamination. Paxton's purer community; Stanley.
Leader cultThe leader as uniquely capable savior, embodiment of the nation, or object of loyalty and devotion. Paxton; Eco's selective populism.
Anti-democratic delegitimizationCourts, elections or the press called illegitimate or "fake"; election results rejected; calls to jail opponents or revoke licenses. Levitsky and Ziblatt's four indicators.
Violence as virtueForce celebrated for its own sake, relish in threats or brutality, or endorsed violence against domestic groups. Paxton's beauty of violence; Eco's permanent war.
Victimhood and retributionThe in-group or leader as persecuted victim, especially when victimhood justifies revenge. Paxton; Stanley.

How the scores are made. Claude coded 1,100 posts on the seven markers blind to account and date, shuffled across every account and administration: an 800-post stratified sample, half random and half chosen on earlier text scores, then 200 and 100 posts chosen on model scores, to add rare positives and to check the highest-scoring government posts from every administration for false alarms. For each marker, a student model estimates the probability the marker is present (logistic regression on word and character n-grams, averaged with gradient boosting on embeddings) times its typical strength; the summed index is calibrated to the coder's scale. It scored all posts. Choosing which posts to code on their text does not bias what the model learns about text.

Validation and examples. Agreement with the coder, marker by marker and group by group, is in Method and data; the highest-scoring posts in each group are in Explore.

What to keep in mind. The coding rules apply to everyone. Calling opponents an existential threat to democracy, or a court illegitimate, counts whoever says it. Routine enforcement posts about one person are not coded as dehumanization unless they generalize to a group. Factual reports of military strikes are not coded as violence unless they relish it. The rare markers (violence, leader cult) have few positive examples, so their scores are noisier than the index. These are machine scores validated against an LLM coder, not against human coders yet.

Measurement

How the scores were made

The design follows Preserving Policy Through Narrative Transformation: a capable teacher model scores a sample against written anchors, and a fast student model trained on those scores rates the full corpus.

  • Teacher labels. Claude coded 1,400 posts in a first round and 300 more from the agency and White House accounts added later, stratified by account type and by before and after January 2025, on six −1 to +1 dimensions plus a villain and a topic. Text was shown blind to account, date and engagement, shuffled across sources, using the codebook's anchors at −1, 0 and +1.
  • Student models. One ridge regression and one gradient-boosted model per dimension, averaged, using word and character TF-IDF, GloVe-Twitter embeddings, VADER sentiment and style features (capital letters, exclamation marks, insults, superlatives, legal boilerplate). The table below gives five-fold cross-validated agreement with the teacher labels.
  • Independent ideology check. A separate classifier trained only on about 124,000 tweets by members of Congress (2017–2023), labeled by party, gives each post a party-language slant. It shares no labels with the teacher, and names and places are masked so it cannot score by who is mentioned.
  • Two of the dimensions map onto legal standards. Partisanship tracks the Hatch Act and GAO's "purely partisan" test. Personalization tracks GAO's "self-aggrandizement" test under the appropriations ban on publicity or propaganda.
IdeologyLiberal (−1) to conservative (+1) policy content and values. Apolitical service content is 0.
ProfessionalismInsults, shouting, mockery and grievance (−1) to formal, precise, institutional register (+1).
PartisanshipAttacks or credits a party, or makes an electoral appeal (−1), to civic and unifying (+1).
Threat or hopeDanger, enemies and blame (−1) to achievement, benefit and gratitude (+1): the Narrative Policy Framework's devil–angel shift.
PersonalizationCenters the leader's persona, praise, crowds or grievances (−1), to centers the institution, policy and public (+1).
FunctionPersuasion, spin and campaign messaging (−1) to alerts, deadlines and factual announcements (+1).

What is missing, and how to add it

This build used only sources the session's network allowed: public GitHub repositories. The pipeline re-runs end to end when more data is added, and the trained scoring carries over.

  • Presidential documents, 2000 to now. The Compilation of Presidential Documents on govinfo.gov covers every statement, proclamation and set of remarks from Bush through Trump's second term. It would extend the presidential series back to 2000 on a comparable official channel.
  • Obama, Biden and second-term Trump institutional accounts. NARA's presidential library social media archives hold @POTUS44, @WhiteHouse44, the Biden accounts and the Trump-era @WhiteHouse45 exports.
  • Agencies outside the archive's scope. DHS, ICE, CBP, USCIS, Justice, FBI, War, Labor, FEMA, the Coast Guard and the White House are now covered. HHS, Treasury, Education, Energy, Interior, Agriculture and EPA still need an X data source (the paid X API, a third-party archive such as twitterapi.io, or Internet Archive captures).
  • Human validation. A coding packet of 120 posts with the codebook is in the data branch, for two human coders to score.
